Oxygen sensor codes P0130 to P0135 and how to match a sensor

Published 2026-11-20 · 7 min read · English version is the reference

An oxygen sensor is the most frequently replaced part that did not need replacing. This guide shows what the code is actually accusing, how to prove it in ten minutes, and which four details lock down the part number once you do.

Upstream and downstream do different jobs

The upstream sensor, before the catalyst, is what the engine management uses to trim fuel. The downstream sensor, after the catalyst, only watches whether the catalyst is still working. Confusing the two is the most common ordering mistake: they often have the same thread and a different cable length, connector or heater rating, and they are not interchangeable.

What the codes are pointing at

CodeReads asFirst thing to check
P0130O2 sensor circuit malfunction (bank 1, sensor 1)The circuit as a whole — connector and harness before the sensor
P0131 / P0132Circuit low voltage / high voltageA lean or rich condition can set both; check for a vacuum leak and fuel pressure before the sensor
P0133Circuit slow responseA genuinely aged sensor — this one usually does need the part
P0134Circuit no activityOpen circuit, dead heater or a sensor that never warms up
P0135Heater circuit malfunction (bank 1, sensor 1)Heater resistance and its fuse; the sensor may still read fine
P0136 – P0141The same family for bank 1 sensor 2Downstream sensor and its heater

Two rules save money here. First, a code that names a sensor does not prove the sensor is faulty — a leaking inlet gasket produces a lean mixture and a P0131 within a few miles. Second, swap the sensors side to side on a V engine and see whether the fault follows the part or the bank. Ten minutes, and it settles the question.

Test before you replace

  1. Look at live data: does the upstream signal switch at all? A signal frozen near 0.45 V is a dead sensor or an open circuit.
  2. Check the heater circuit: resistance between the two heater wires, then voltage at the connector with the key on. A blown heater fuse takes out both sensors on some platforms.
  3. Spray brake cleaner around the inlet and vacuum hoses while watching the fuel trims — a jump means an air leak, not a sensor.
  4. Inspect the cable where it passes the heat shield; chafing there is a classic intermittent.
  5. Check that the sensor is the right one for the position — an upstream fitted downstream reads plausibly for a while, then sets a catalyst code.

The four details that lock the part number

  • Position. Bank 1 or bank 2, upstream or downstream — four different numbers on a V6.
  • Wire count and connector. A 4-wire and a 6-wire sensor are different generations; the connector keyway differs too.
  • Cable length. The same sensor with 150 mm more cable is a different OE number, and the short one will not reach the clip.
  • Thread and reach. M18 × 1.5 is the norm, but a long-reach body will bottom out before it seals on some manifolds.

Universal "splice-in" sensors are cheaper, and for a workshop with the right crimping tool they are fine. For export orders they are usually the wrong answer: the customer wants the connector, the length and the bracket to be identical to what came off the car, because a returned part costs more than the saving.
