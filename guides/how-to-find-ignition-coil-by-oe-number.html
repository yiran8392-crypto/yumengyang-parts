How to find the right ignition coil by OE number

Published 2026-11-02 · 6 min read · English version is the reference

Engine code, not car model, decides which coil fits. This guide shows how to start from the number stamped on the old coil, read it correctly, and convert it into a list of numbers you can actually order against.

Why the OE number beats the car model

Two cars can share a badge and take different coils. A Volkswagen Golf with a 1.6 petrol engine and a Golf with a 1.4 TSI use different coil families, and within one engine there are often two revisions that are not interchangeable. The workshop that orders "a coil for a 2012 Golf" has maybe a 70% chance of getting the right part the first time. The workshop that orders against the number on the old coil has close to 100%.

The OE number is the manufacturer's own identifier for that exact assembly: housing height, connector keyway, pin count, built-in igniter or not, and the length of the boot. Those are the things that stop a coil from seating, not the engine size.

How an ignition coil OE number is built

Every major brand encodes the same information in a different shape. Learn the shape and you can read an unfamiliar number in a few seconds.

Two habits save time here. First, strip the spaces and dashes before you search: "0 221 504 465", "0221504465" and "0221-504-465" are one number. Second, keep the trailing letter — 06A905115 and 06A905115D are different parts even though the first eight characters match.

Where to read the number on the car

  • Laser marking on the coil body — usually on the flat top or the side of the housing, small and often covered by dust. Wipe it and use a torch at an angle.
  • Moulded label or sticker on the connector housing; on older coils this is the only place the revision letter appears.
  • The rubber boot or the metal bracket, where some makers stamp a short code instead of the full number.
  • The workshop parts catalogue (ETKA, EPC, Microcat) opened by VIN — this gives the number the factory fitted, which is what you want for a car that has never been repaired.
  • The old invoice or the box, if the coil has already been replaced once. Beware: the box may hold a cheaper substitute, not the original.

If the marking is unreadable, photograph the connector and the boot next to a ruler and send the pictures with the VIN. A coil is identified visually faster than it is guessed from a model name.

From one number to a cross-reference list

A single OE number usually has three or four equivalents. Suppliers renumber, and the car maker supersedes a part without changing the casting. Work the chain in this order:

  1. Write down the number from the old part exactly, including the revision letter.
  2. Look up the supersession: 06A905115 became 06A905115D, and later D was replaced again. Later letters are normally backward compatible; earlier letters are not always forward compatible.
  3. Collect the equivalent numbers from two or three other brands for the same engine — that is what a cross-reference list is.
  4. Confirm the physical details against the listing: pin count, connector shape, boot length, and whether the coil has an integrated igniter.
  5. Only then compare price. Price without the cross-reference check is how wrong parts get shipped.

On our site every coil page carries an OE number and, where we have it, a cross-reference row — for example Bosch 0221503465 lists the vehicles it fits, and the full ignition coil catalogue can be filtered by OE number, our SKU or vehicle name.

Five mistakes that cause wrong shipments

  • Cylinder-specific coils. Some engines use the same electrical coil in two housing lengths. The number differs by one letter and the long one will not seat under the cover.
  • Pin count and keyway. Three-pin and four-pin coils look identical in a photo. Count the pins and check the keyway before ordering.
  • Igniter inside or outside. A coil with a built-in igniter cannot replace one that uses an external module, even if the connector fits.
  • Marketplace "OE numbers". A listing ID is not an OE number. If the number does not appear on the part, treat it as a description, not an identifier.
  • Look-alike characters. Zero and letter O, one and letter I, and the letters B and 8 are the usual culprits on laser marking. When a search returns nothing, re-read the number character by character.

The 60-second check before you order

  1. Scan or read the fault code and confirm which cylinder is misfiring — do not replace all four coils because of one code.
  2. Read the number off the failed coil and write it down with the revision letter.
  3. Search that number on our OE and cross-reference lookup and open the matching part page.
  4. Check fitment, pin count and boot length against the old part photo; compare the cross-reference row.
  5. Send the OE number and the quantity on WhatsApp for price, MOQ and packing.

If the number you have returns nothing, send it anyway with the VIN and a photo of the connector. Numbers that are not in a catalogue are usually superseded numbers or a dealer-only revision, and both can be matched by hand. To place the car from its VIN first, see VIN to parts.
