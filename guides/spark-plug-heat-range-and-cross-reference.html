Spark plug heat range, reach and cross-reference explained

Published 2026-11-20 · 6 min read · English version is the reference

A spark plug is the cheapest part on the invoice and the easiest to get wrong. Same thread, same hex, different heat range — and the engine either fouls plugs or knocks itself apart. This guide covers the four dimensions that decide the part number, and how to cross-reference between brands without guessing.

The four dimensions that must all match

DimensionCommon valuesWhat happens if it is wrong
ThreadM14 × 1.25, M12 × 1.25, M10 × 1.0Will not screw in at all, or strips the head
Reach12.7 mm, 19 mm, 26.5 mmToo short fouls and loses power; too long hits the piston
SeatGasket (crush washer) or taper (no washer)The plug sits at the wrong depth or leaks compression
Heat rangeNumbered differently by each brandToo hot pre-ignites and can hole a piston; too cold fouls and misfires

Reading a heat-range number

Every brand numbers heat range in its own direction. Learning the direction prevents the most expensive mistake in this list.

BrandNumberingExample
NGKHigher number = colderBKR6E is medium; BKR7E is one step colder; BKR5E one step hotter
DensoHigher number = colder (same direction as NGK)K20R-U11 is medium
BoschHigher number = hotterFR7DC+ is hotter than FR6DC+ — the opposite of NGK
ChampionHigher number = hotterRC9YC is hotter than RC7YC

A plug one step colder is the normal fix for a turbocharged or LPG-converted engine that is otherwise standard. Two steps and the plug starts fouling in town driving.

Electrode material and gap

  • Nickel plugs are the baseline and need gapping and frequent replacement.
  • Platinum and iridium last longer and are pre-gapped; never re-gap an iridium plug by levering the centre electrode.
  • Turbo and direct-injection engines are sensitive to gap: an eroded gap of 1.3 mm where 0.7 mm is specified produces a high-load misfire with no fault code.
  • Resistor type matters on modern cars — a non-resistor plug can upset the ignition module and the radio.
  • LPG and CNG want a colder, harder plug than petrol; the same car on gas will foul the standard plug.

How to cross-reference without guessing

  1. Start from the OE number in the parts catalogue, not from the plug that came out — the old plug may already be wrong.
  2. Decode the plug you have: thread, reach, seat and heat range, in that order.
  3. Compare heat-range directions between brands before swapping numbers; a "7" in Bosch is not the same position as a "7" in NGK.
  4. Check the seat type last — a taper-seat plug in a gasket-seat head will not seal.
  5. Verify against the vehicle's OE list on our part page before shipping a bulk order.

Cross-reference tables are a starting point, not a warranty. The OE number of the car is the only thing that settles a claim.
