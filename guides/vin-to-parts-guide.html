VIN to parts: how to read a VIN and find the exact part

Published 2026-11-02 · 7 min read · English version is the reference

A VIN identifies one vehicle, not one part. Read the four blocks correctly and the VIN removes most of the guesswork — then confirm the last step with an OE number.

What a VIN actually is

Since 1981 every road vehicle sold in the United States carries a 17-character Vehicle Identification Number, and the format is now used worldwide under ISO 3779. It is fixed length, contains no spaces, and never uses the letters I, O or Q — they are excluded so they cannot be confused with 1 and 0.

That last detail matters in practice. If a customer sends a "VIN" containing the letter O, it is either a typing error or not a VIN at all.

The four blocks

CharactersNameWhat it gives you
1–3WMI — World Manufacturer IdentifierWho built the vehicle and in which country. This is the block that tells you the brand.
4–8VDS — Vehicle Descriptor SectionMaker-defined: body style, series, engine family, restraint system. Meaning changes completely between brands.
9Check digitA calculated character. Used to catch typos before anyone orders a part.
10Model yearOne letter or digit for the model year — not the registration year.
11Plant codeWhich factory assembled the vehicle. Useful when the same model was built in two countries with different suppliers.
12–17Serial numberThe sequence number. Unique within the plant and year.

Note the Chinese and Brazilian WMIs. A "German" model assembled in China or Brazil often uses locally sourced sensors and ignition parts, so the WMI is not trivia — it decides which parts list applies. Used imports from the Gulf, Africa and Latin America frequently fall into this group.

Character 10: the model year code

Position 10 is a single letter or digit. The letters I, O, Q, U and Z are skipped, and the cycle repeats every 30 years — which is why a 1990 car and a 2020 car can both read "L".

The model year is not the registration year. A car first registered in March 2011 is very often a 2010 model year, and facelift parts change on the model-year boundary, not on the calendar.

What a VIN does not tell you

  • The engine code. Two cars with the same VIN prefix can carry different engines, and the coil, sensor or pump changes with the engine.
  • Brake and suspension package. Bigger brakes mean a different ABS sensor bracket and cable length.
  • Whether the car has been re-engined, converted or rebuilt after an accident — common on used imports.
  • Left- or right-hand drive on models built in both configurations; the ABS sensor position is mirrored and the part numbers differ.

So the VIN narrows the search; the OE number closes it. Treat the VIN as the first filter and the part number as the confirmation.

The workflow we use

  1. Paste the 17 characters into the VIN box on our lookup home page. It reports the brand from the WMI, the model year from position 10, and whether the check digit is valid.
  2. Match the vehicle to the engine: ask for the engine code, or the displacement and fuel type from the registration document.
  3. Open the category page — for example ABS sensors or ignition coils — and filter by vehicle name.
  4. Confirm the OE number against the old part, then use the cross-reference row on the part page to see equivalent numbers.
  5. Send the OE number, quantity and destination port on WhatsApp for the quotation.

A worked example

VIN WVWZZZ1K9AW123456 reads as: WVW = Volkswagen Germany, ZZZ = filler used when the VDS is not needed, 1K = the Golf Mk6 platform, check digit 9 valid, A = model year 2010. The car is a 2010 Golf, but the engine is still unknown — and that is the point.

If the job is a front-left ABS sensor on that car, the OE number is WHT003857 (cross-referenced to 7H0927803 on many listings). Our catalogue carries it as WHT003857 / 7H0927803, with the front-right counterpart listed separately. Same VIN, same axle, different side, different number — that is the whole lesson of this guide.
